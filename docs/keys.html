⚠️ Controversy & Criticisms

This section summarizes common criticisms so readers can interpret the data appropriately.

Allan Lichtman's "13 Keys to the White House" system has faced significant criticism and accusations of overfitting and pseudoscience. While Lichtman claims a near-perfect prediction record, critics raise several concerns. This is an excellent article on this topic by The Post Rider.

I have also written my own analysis here.

Key criticisms include:

  • Popular Vote vs. Electoral College: The keys have always predicted the popular vote winner, rather than the electoral college. You can see that in the above analysis that shows the keys retrospectively predicted Samuel J. Tilden in 1876 and Grover Cleveland in 1888, both of whom won the popular vote but lost the electoral college. Indeed, in 2000 Lichtman predicted Al Gore who won the popular vote but lost the electoral college to George W. Bush. Even in October 2016, Lichtman published an article predicting Donald Trump but also stating the keys predict only the popular vote. After Trump won the electoral college, and lost the popular vote, Lichtman claimed the keys had been changed since 2000 to predict the electoral college winner, contradicting his earlier statements. If anyone tells you the keys predict the electoral college, even if that is Allan Lichtman himself, they are rewriting history, contradicting what Allan Lichtman has said previously, and even what is printed in the 2024 edition of his book, "Predicting the Next President". See this article for more details.
  • 2016 Controversy: Lichtman predicted Trump would win in 2016, but Hillary Clinton won the popular vote by 2.1%. Based on writings as late as October 2016, Lichtman stated the keys predicted only the popular vote winner. Given that context, this means the keys, as called by Lichtman, were wrong in 2016.
  • Overfitting the data: Critics argue the keys were developed by retroactively fitting too closely to the historical election outcomes, rather than being based on robust predictive factors. That is, they look great on past data but may not generalize well to future elections. Critics point to 2016 and 2024 as examples where the system failed to predict the outcome correctly, based on its stated criteria of predicting the popular vote winner.
  • Unweighted Keys: All keys are treated equally, but acknowledged as having varying weight in different elections. Therefore, a simple 6-key threshold as a deterministic rule for the incumbent party losing the popular vote is mathematically incoherent. That would require each key to have equal predictive power in order for such a threshold to work deterministically.
  • Subjective Key Assignments: Many keys require subjective judgment (e.g., "incumbent charisma," "scandal," "social unrest"). Lichtman often assigns these values after an election's outcome is known, and he has been accused of changing key assignments retroactively to preserve his record. See this article by Nate Silver for more details.
  • Small Sample Size: With only ~40 elections since 1860, statistical validation is limited. The model as defined failed in 2016 and 2024, when it failed to predict the popular vote winner.
  • Lack of Quantitative Thresholds: Unlike statistical models with defined probability outputs, the keys provide binary predictions with no uncertainty estimates, making them difficult to evaluate rigorously.

This analysis presents the keys alongside actual election data so you can evaluate the system's predictive power for yourself.
